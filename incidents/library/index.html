AI incident library and VAP mapping

This library lists every record on the incident verification records page and maps each one to the questions about evidence that it raised. For each question it sets out what VAP v1.2 makes auditable and attributable, what the current specification does not address, and the related candidate work, none of which has been adopted. It is a reading aid. The verification records remain the authority for what was verified, and nothing here assumes that VAP was in use in any incident.

How to read this page

  • Areas and the labels T1–T13 are VSO’s editorial classification. They are navigation aids, not registry fields, ledger entries or identifiers in the VAP specification.
  • A record can raise several questions. Assigning a question is VSO’s reading of the record, not a finding about any party.
  • Litigation: every claim made in a case is an untested allegation unless a court has decided it. Each record is read under its own jurisdiction only.
  • Where a record describes VAP’s relevance, it is a design inference, not an implemented capability. “Not in VAP v1.2” describes the current text; related candidate work is listed so that readers can follow it, and none of it is adopted or scheduled for a release.
  • Records are added here when they are added to the verification records. The build that publishes both pages fails if a record has not been classified.

What the records show so far

  1. The records almost always existed. In most incidents an operator, a developer or an affected party held logs and used them to reconstruct what happened.
  2. What outsiders could not check recurs: whose record it was (T1: 23 records), whether a review found everything (T2: 8 records), what was in scope and observed (T3: 22 records), whether requests, alerts and remedies took effect (T4: 16 records), and whether a valid credential meant authorisation (T5: 23 records).
  3. In VSO’s reading of these 85 records, none turned on what the core of VAP v1.2 is built to detect: omission or alteration of anchored records by the party holding them, after anchoring (INT-008). Where records were erased, it was the actor erasing records that had not been anchored. The case these records make is therefore for parts that are not yet in the specification.
  4. The number of records is not evidence of need. VSO chose which reports to check, the choice leans towards cases that look relevant to VAP, and many records come from a few disclosing companies.

  1. T1 · 23 records

    Whose record was it?

    Accounts rested on one party's records — often the acting agent's own — and outsiders could not tell a self-report from an independent observation.

    What VAP v1.2 makes auditable

    A tamper-evident record shows that entries were not altered after they were written and anchored (§4.1; INT-004, INT-006), and a signature binds a record to a key (INT-005, §6.1.2). Where parties arrange it in advance, cross-party provenance (XREF, TRC-005) lets corresponding records held by different parties be checked against each other.

    Not in VAP v1.2

    That an entry was true when it was written: a record of a spoofed tool call is kept as faithfully as a true one. v1.2 has no field that distinguishes an actor's own account from a harness observation, a counterparty's record or a record imported from a party that did not take part in XREF.

  2. T2 · 8 records

    Did the review find everything?

    A developer or operator reviewed its own records and reported what it found. Later disclosures showed that earlier reviews had missed incidents, and no outsider could see which records had been searched.

    What VAP v1.2 makes auditable

    For any anchored batch, omission of in-scope events after anchoring, and split-view presentation, are detectable by a verifier holding the anchor (Completeness Invariant, INT-008; §4.1.7) — at anchor time and batch granularity only.

    Not in VAP v1.2

    Which records a retrospective review covered and how it searched them. An event never included in an anchored batch is not revealed by INT-008 alone; it can be shown to be missing only by reference to another record that implies it (§11.1), and an event that was never measured is out of reach (Appendix A, Pre-measurement drop).

  3. T3 · 22 records

    What was in scope, and what was observed?

    What a system or agent could reach, what was being monitored and where logging stopped were stated after the event, if at all.

    What VAP v1.2 makes auditable

    INT-008 uses the term “declared scope” and requires each AnchorRecord to bind the event count, the first and last event identifiers and the policy identifier under which the batch was produced (§7.5); a verifier holding the anchor can check those bindings.

    Not in VAP v1.2

    “Declared scope” is not defined, and nothing requires it to be committed before the events it covers. There is no way to mark a resource declared reachable but not instrumented, or to record a change in monitoring conditions as such.

  4. T4 · 16 records

    Did the request, alert or remedy take effect?

    Deletion requests, alerts, key rotations, recalls and stop orders were reported as sent or done. Whether the other side received them, and whether an effect was observed, were separate facts that the accounts ran together.

    What VAP v1.2 makes auditable

    Recorded actions and their outcome status (§4.2.2) are tamper-evident once anchored, and their omission after anchoring is detectable (INT-008). Where a profile or capability requires a recorded request or attempt to terminate in a recorded outcome, a missing outcome can be shown against that requirement (§11.1).

    Not in VAP v1.2

    v1.2 has no common rule that requests, alerts or recommendations close with a terminating event, and does not separate a request, its receipt, the other party's claim and an observed effect. The passage of time, or a reply, is not an observation of effect.

  5. T5 · 23 records

    A valid credential is not an authorisation

    Genuine identifiers, valid keys and accepted credentials were used for actions their holders did not authorise, or an approved action differed from the one carried out.

    What VAP v1.2 makes auditable

    A signature binds a record to a key (INT-005, §6.1.2). The Accountability Layer records the operator, the last approval and a delegation chain with scope and validity period (§4.4.3).

    Not in VAP v1.2

    “Attributable” is not defined beyond verification under a supplied key. A valid signature or credential does not show that the operation was authorised, who or what controlled the key, or that what was executed matched what was approved, and v1.2 does not ask an assessment to report these questions separately.

  6. T6 · 6 records

    Code that ran before, or with, the recording point

    Malicious code ran before any logging point inside the affected component, or with the same privileges as its records and keys, so the compromised party's own records and signatures could not separate genuine activity from the attacker's.

    What VAP v1.2 makes auditable

    Records anchored before a compromise can still be checked against their anchors (INT-006), so a later alteration of them is detectable.

    Not in VAP v1.2

    v1.2 defines no time-stamped declaration that a signing key was compromised, and does not require recording keys and anchoring credentials to sit outside the trust boundary of the actor being recorded.

  7. T7 · 6 records

    A published judgement changed later

    An organisation's dated assessment — of scope, exposure, attribution or classification — was later revised, and readers had to work out which statement replaced which.

    What VAP v1.2 makes auditable

    Anchored records are tamper-evident, so a dated statement that was recorded and anchored cannot be rewritten later without detection. Overrides of an action are recorded in override_history (§4.4.3).

    Not in VAP v1.2

    There is no type that links a later judgement to the earlier one it replaces, keeps it distinct from the original, and separates a recorded replacement from evidence that the replacement was carried out.

  8. T8 · 9 records

    Secret values in records

    Passwords, tokens and API keys ended up in logs, build histories, task records or shared evidence, so the records themselves became a path of exposure.

    What VAP v1.2 makes auditable

    ERASURE records crypto-shredding of protected fields that the implementation itself encrypted, leaving hashes and anchors verifiable (§6.3, §7.4).

    Not in VAP v1.2

    No rule keeps reusable secrets out of signed or anchored records. Once anchored, a plaintext secret cannot be withdrawn, and crypto-shredding covers only what was encrypted before it went in.

  9. T9 · 39 records

    No domain profile fits

    The deployment — an evaluation environment, a general-purpose assistant or coding agent, a consumer agent, an employment or moderation system, a vehicle — falls outside every registered domain profile.

    What VAP v1.2 makes auditable

    A deployment invokes capabilities alongside exactly one domain profile (§4.6.3, rule 1). The profile registry lists VCP (finance), CAP (content) and CPP (capture) as released, and OAP, MAP and PAP as drafts; DVP (automotive) is listed as Planned, a status that means no specification document exists (§4.5.2).

    Not in VAP v1.2

    A conformance route for a deployment that no registered profile covers. VAP is not a general-purpose logging framework (§1.3); a design inference in such a record is not a conformance claim. Where the specification names the domain but its profile is only planned or a partial draft (transport, §2.2.3; public sector, §2.2.5), what is missing is the profile itself; the candidate row below concerns deployments with no registered domain.

  11. T11 · 6 records

    Was the human step adequate?

    The failure lay in people and procedure: an output nobody checked, a review that stopped at the edge of an institution's records, or human involvement whose adequacy is a legal question.

    What VAP v1.2 makes auditable

    A recorded step, including a human review where the deployment records one, can be made attributable (§4.4.3: operator_id, last_approval_by; §4.4.4).

    Not in VAP v1.2

    Whether a review was adequate, a policy sound or a response timely. VAP does not itself provide monitoring, intervention or override capability (§4.4.4), and does not warrant the correctness, fairness or safety of the decisions recorded (§1.6(c)).

VAP v1.2 §1.6 (verbatim)

VAP and its domain profiles define mechanisms for producing cryptographically verifiable evidence of AI system decisions. Conformance to VAP or any profile: (a) does not constitute compliance with the EU AI Act, GDPR, MiFID II/III, CAT Rule 613, NIS2, FDA SaMD guidance, or any other law or regulation; (b) does not constitute a legal determination that any technical mechanism (including crypto-shredding) satisfies a specific legal obligation; (c) does not warrant the correctness, fairness, or safety of the underlying AI decisions — only the integrity, completeness (at anchor granularity), and attributability of their records. VAP generates evidence; competent authorities and courts evaluate it.
